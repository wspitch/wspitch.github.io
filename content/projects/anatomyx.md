## At a glance
**Role.** Project lead on a 5-person research team with a faculty advisor, responsible for the research end to end. AnatomyX sponsored the project.
**Question.** What should the app’s team fix first?
**Method.** An expert heuristic evaluation, then a usability test with 10 people on HoloLens 2, so the user test could check whether the experts’ predictions held for real learners.
**Findings.** Only half of participants managed to pin an item, and the cursor stopped lining up with people’s hands. Both methods pointed to tutorial overload, small objects, and weak selection feedback.
**Impact.** A ranked shortlist in 3 areas, which the AnatomyX product team used to build their own design guidelines for AR interactions.

## Why interaction is the risk
The app lets students view and manipulate a virtual human body. On a headset, learners must acquire a new set of gestures before they can learn any anatomy, so an interaction problem becomes a learning problem.

## Findings

### First impressions were strong, but hands and cursor disagreed
*“Very impressive! I like the details.”* Then the cursor stopped lining up with where people’s hands were. People wanted to grab the whole eye, and it came apart into pieces.

### Pinning was the hardest task
Only half of participants managed to pin an item, and it had the highest difficulty rating of any task (3.3 out of 5). Many did it by accident or looked for a button. One said, *“It says to press and hold, but I don’t really know what it means.”* People also couldn’t tell what was selected, so they repeated gestures and grabbed the wrong part.

### Features lived where people did not look
One participant found the “Structures” menu only because our task wording said “structures,” a reminder that our own tasks can give things away. The tutorial asked too much of people: *“That was a lot of information, oh boy.”* Another found it helpful, *“although not as helpful as playing around with it.”*

Triangulating the expert evaluation with the user test, both methods converged on the biggest problems: tutorial overload, small objects that were hard to see against a busy room, and weak feedback on what was selected. We prioritized those.

## Deciding what to report
Between the expert review and 10 sessions, we had a long list of issues. Reporting all of them would have left the sponsor’s team to decide where to start, so I proposed a prioritization rule before we debated individual findings. Problems in 2 groups went first:

- Problems that both the expert evaluation and the user test found.
- Problems that stopped people from finishing a task.

Everything else went in the full log. Agreeing on criteria first kept the discussion on how serious a problem was and away from debating each issue on its own.

## Impact
- The AnatomyX product team took our findings into consideration and used our recommendations to build their own design guidelines for interface design for AR interactions.
- The team received a ranked shortlist in 3 areas, the tutorial, design and consistency, and accessibility, instead of a long list. The full issue log went in an appendix.
- It also gained a baseline to improve on. Usability scored 62.0, below the common benchmark of 68, and likelihood to recommend was 33. With 10 participants, I read both as directional.

## What I ruled out
- Reporting every issue. That would have left the sponsor’s team to decide where to start, so the full log went in an appendix.
- Debating each finding on its own. I proposed a prioritization rule first, so the discussion stayed on how serious a problem was.
- Trusting the expert review alone. It is fast but rests on expert judgment, so the user test checked whether the experts’ predictions held for real learners.

## What I’d do differently
I would bring in anatomy students in a real class. 10 participants in a lab show where the interaction breaks, and only a classroom can show whether the fixes hold up in actual learning.
