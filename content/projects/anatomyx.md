## At a glance
**Role.** Project lead on a five-person research team with a faculty advisor, responsible for the research end to end.
**Methods.** An expert heuristic evaluation, then a usability test with 10 people (eight scenario tasks, about 70 minutes each), with standardized measures for usability, experience, simulator sickness, exertion, and likelihood to recommend.
**The decision.** What the app's team should fix first.

## Why interaction matters here
The app lets students view and manipulate a virtual human body. On a headset, learners have to pick up a new set of gestures before they can learn any anatomy. If the gestures fight them, the learning stops.

## What we found
First impressions were strong: *"Very impressive! I like the details."* But the cursor didn't always line up with where people's hands were. People wanted to grab the whole eye, and it came apart into pieces.

Only half of participants managed to pin an item, and pinning had the highest difficulty rating of any task (3.3 out of 5). Many did it by accident or looked for a button. One said: *"It says to press and hold, but I don't really know what it means."* People also couldn't tell what was selected, so they repeated gestures and grabbed the wrong part.

Features lived where people didn't look. One participant told us she only found the "Structures" menu because our task wording said "structures," a reminder that our own tasks can give things away. The tutorial was too much: *"That was a lot of information, oh boy."* Another found it helpful, *"although not as helpful as playing around with it."*

The expert evaluation and the user test agreed on the biggest problems: tutorial overload, small objects that were hard to see against a busy room, and weak feedback on what was selected. That's why those went first.

## Deciding what to report
Between the expert review and ten sessions, we ended up with a long list of issues, and the easy move was to report all of them. I wanted the app's team to know where to start, so I proposed a simple rule: problems that both the expert evaluation and the user test found, and problems that stopped people from finishing a task, went first. Everything else went in the full log. That kept the conversation on the rule instead of on each individual issue.

## Impact
- A ranked shortlist, not a long list, from the evaluation plan through to the final recommendations, in three areas: the tutorial, design and consistency, and accessibility. The full issue log went in an appendix.
- A baseline to beat. Usability scored 62.0, below the common benchmark of 68, and likelihood to recommend was 33. With ten participants, I'd read both as directional.

## What I'd do differently
I'd bring in anatomy students in a real class. Ten participants in a lab tell you where the interaction breaks, but a classroom would tell you how the app lives in actual use.
