## At a glance
**Role.** UX researcher on the lab team.
**Timeline.** 2021 to 2022.
**Methods.** Controlled lab experiments where each person tried a range of surface temperatures, touch durations, and room temperatures, with ratings and observed actions.
**The decision.** How warm a surface can get before people stop being comfortable touching it.

## A note on this page
The company said I can’t share anything data related, so there are no temperatures or results here, and the company and products aren’t named. What’s left is how we thought about the problem, how we studied it, and what I’d do differently.

## The problem
Choosing a material by feel is expensive in both directions. If a team is too cautious, designers lose room to work. If it’s too bold, people get uncomfortable or hurt. People also differ a lot in what feels hot, so a single number from a small sample wouldn’t hold up.

## How we studied it
The question needed control, so we ran lab studies where surface temperature, touch duration, and room temperature could be set precisely. Each person tried a range of conditions, which let us compare them with themselves. We kept the design the same across both materials so the results could sit side by side.

Safety shaped the design too. We couldn’t hold people on the hottest surfaces for long, so those conditions were short touches only. We said so in every readout and didn’t guess past what we’d observed.

## What people did
We tracked what people did as well as what they said, and the actions told us more than the ratings. As surfaces got warmer, people noticed, then investigated, then pulled away. Some imagined what they’d do at home: turn the device off, move it into the shade, call tech support, or send it back.

Age added a twist. Older participants worried more about safety but took fewer steps to investigate or fix the problem. The room mattered as well. **Comfort depended on who was touching, for how long, and in what room**, which is why we gave ranges and not one number.

## Impact
- The findings fed evidence-based material decisions on smart-home hardware.
- I presented the work to stakeholders across research, design, engineering, and product.

## What I’d do differently
Two things bothered me. On one material, the placement of the temperature sensor pushed people to touch the surface in an unnatural way. And everyone did the cooler room first and the warmer room second, so order and room temperature were tangled together. In one study, participants also had to imagine a device and couldn’t hold one.

Next time I’d match the setup to real touch, counterbalance the room conditions, and add a follow-up that feels more like home.
