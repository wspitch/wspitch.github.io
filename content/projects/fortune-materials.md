## At a glance
**Role.** UX researcher on the lab team.
**Timeline.** 2021 to 2022.
**Methods.** Controlled lab experiments where each person tried a range of surface temperatures, touch durations, and room temperatures, with ratings and observed actions.
**The decision.** How warm a surface can get before people stop being comfortable touching it.

## A note on this page
Temperatures, results, and the company are left out here, and I’d rather say so than give you a vague summary. What’s left is how we thought about it, what we learned about people, and what I’d do differently.

## The problem
Choosing a material by feel is expensive in both directions. Too cautious, and designers lose room to work. Too bold, and people get uncomfortable or worse. People also differ a lot in what feels hot, so a single number from a small sample wouldn’t hold up.

## How we studied it
The question needed control, so we ran lab studies where surface temperature, touch duration, and room temperature could be set precisely. Each person tried a range of conditions, so we could compare them with themselves. We kept the design the same across both materials so the results could sit side by side.

Safety shaped the design too. We couldn’t hold people on the hottest surfaces for long, so the hottest conditions were short touches only, and we said so in every readout instead of guessing past what we’d observed.

## What people did
We tracked what people did as well as what they said, and the actions told us more than the ratings. As surfaces got warmer, people noticed, then investigated, then pulled away. Some imagined what they’d do at home: turn the device off, move it into the shade, call tech support, or send it back.

Age added a twist. Older participants were more worried about safety, but they took fewer steps to investigate or fix the problem. A warmer room also made short touches feel worse. So comfort depended on temperature, touch duration, the room, and who was touching, which is why we gave ranges, not one number.

## Impact
- The findings fed evidence-based material decisions on smart-home hardware.
- I presented the results to stakeholders across research, design, engineering, and product.

## What I’d do differently
Two things bothered me. On one material, the placement of the temperature sensor pushed people to touch the surface in an unnatural way. And everyone did the cooler room first and the warmer room second, so order and room temperature were tangled together. In one study, participants also had to imagine a device instead of holding one. Next time I’d match the setup to real touch, counterbalance the room conditions, and add a follow-up that feels more like home.
