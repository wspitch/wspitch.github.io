## At a glance
**Role.** User research intern on the Customer Insight and User Research team, which sits in the product team.
**Timeline.** June to August 2026, about two months.
**Methods.** Desk research, a survey of 600+ owners, interviews, and concept tests with a prototype I coded.
**The decision.** Whether to invest in serving micro-business owners, and which owners to build for.

## Where I started
The brief was to explore how the company could serve micro-business owners. Nobody had pinned down the problem, and I had about two months. With that little time, the real risk was answering the wrong questions very well.

So on day one I wrote down every question leadership might ask and ranked them by what the decision needed. Three survived: who has this problem most, what are they trying to get done, and would a concept change how they work. Pricing, features, and feasibility had to wait. None of them could be answered honestly before we knew the need was real.

## What the notebooks said
Desk research framed the space, the survey showed where the need was concentrated, and the interviews changed my mind.

Owners already had a CRM. It was a paper notebook. Page after page of handwriting, in Thai, with what each customer ordered, what they liked, and what went wrong last time. Nothing was missing from those notes. They were scattered, and nothing told the owner when to act on them. Customers who drift away don't announce it. They just stop coming, and by the time you notice, months have passed.

Two ideas helped me read what I was seeing. Schön's reflection-in-action: jotting down a customer's details is thinking on the page, not filing a record. And Polanyi's tacit knowledge: who matters, and who has gone quiet, is something owners know from experience, not from a formula. That kept me asking how owners know things, and stopped me asking what features they wanted.

## The concept, and who it's for
The concept was a mobile-first tool that organizes scattered customer notes (photo, voice, or text) and sends one timely nudge to reconnect before a customer silently leaves. It doesn't replace jotting things down.

The need was strongest in businesses where customers come back after a one-off service. Think pet carers, home bakers, and florists, where the next visit depends on a birthday or a trip. It was weaker where customers are on long contracts, like a regular tutor or a landlord. I wrote that boundary into the recommendation on purpose.

## Testing it with something they could touch
A static concept can't tell you whether someone will adopt a new habit. So I built a working prototype myself and tested it with two groups of owners.

The reactions went beyond what I'd planned for. A pet carer said it would save her from asking owners the same questions over and over, and that owners would feel she actually cared. A baker said that mixing up who is allergic to nuts or milk isn't just awkward, it's dangerous, and that she'd use a reminder to reach out before a customer's usual birthday order went to another shop. Owners of come-back businesses were clearly more enthusiastic than the contract-based group. Both groups were small (7 and 4), so I read the results as a direction, not a verdict.

## The readout
I presented to company leadership, including department heads. The deck walked from the problem, to the concept, to who it is and isn't for, and it ended on the questions I'd left open: which nudges to send, how to structure the notes, whether this is a standalone app or a feature inside a tool owners already use, and how customers would consent to being remembered by software.

## Holding the boundary
When I shared the direction, the natural question was whether we could cover more kinds of businesses, including the contract-based ones, so the concept didn't look so narrow. It's a fair question, and I didn't want to answer it with a flat no. I went back to what the tests said: owners of come-back businesses were clearly more enthusiastic than the contract-based group, and the question on the table was who to build for first. Widening the scope would have blurred that answer. We kept the boundary and logged the other group as a later question.

## Impact
- Leadership reviewed my recommendation and gave the concept a place on the product roadmap for future exploration.
- The recommendation named the owners with the strongest need and left others out on purpose.
- Demand and desirability were tested before anything was built, so the roadmap call rested on what owners said and did.

## What I'd carry forward
Finding the right question was most of the work. The consent question is the one I'd want answered before this ships. It's a product question as much as an ethics one.
