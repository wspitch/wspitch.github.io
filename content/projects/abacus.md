## At a glance
**Role.** User research intern on the Customer Insight and User Research team, which sits in the product team.
**Timeline.** June to August 2026, about two months.
**Methods.** Desk research, a survey of micro-business owners, interviews, and concept tests with a prototype I coded.
**The decision.** Whether to invest in serving micro-business owners, and which owners to build for.

## A note on this page
This work is under NDA, so I'm not sharing findings, the concept, or product details. What I can share is how I worked: how I scoped an open question, where I changed my mind, and what I'd do again. I'm happy to talk through more in conversation.

## Where I started
The brief was to explore how the company could serve micro-business owners. Nobody had pinned down the problem, and I had about two months. With that little time, the real risk was answering the wrong questions very well.

So on day one I wrote down every question leadership might ask and ranked them by what the decision needed. Three survived: who has this problem most, what are they trying to get done, and would a concept change how they work. Pricing, features, and feasibility had to wait. None of them could be answered honestly before we knew the need was real.

## Where I changed my mind
Desk research framed the space, the survey showed where to look, and the interviews changed my mind. I can't share what I heard. What I can share is the lesson: the most useful moment of the summer was when what people told me stopped fitting the story I'd arrived with, and I had to decide whether to trust my plan or the people.

Two ideas helped me read what I was hearing. Schön's reflection-in-action reminded me that people often think by doing, not by filing things away. Polanyi's tacit knowledge reminded me that much of what people know is hard to put into words. That kept me asking how owners know things, and stopped me asking what features they wanted.

## Testing it with something they could touch
A static concept can't tell you whether someone will adopt a new habit. So I built a working prototype myself and tested it with two small groups of owners. The reactions went beyond what I'd planned for, which is the point of putting something real in front of people. Because the groups were small, I read the results as a direction, not a verdict.

## The readout
I presented to company leadership, including department heads. The deck walked from the problem, to the concept, to who it is and isn't for, and it ended on the questions I'd left open instead of pretending they were settled.

## Holding the boundary
When I shared the direction, the natural question was whether we could widen the scope so the concept didn't look so narrow. It's a fair question, and I didn't want to answer it with a flat no. I went back to what the tests said and to the question actually on the table, which was who to build for first. Widening the scope would have blurred that answer. We kept the boundary and logged the others as a later question.

## Impact
- Leadership reviewed my recommendation and gave the concept a place on the product roadmap for future exploration.
- The recommendation was specific about who to serve, and left others out on purpose.
- Demand and desirability were tested before anything was built, so the roadmap call rested on what owners said and did.

## What I'd carry forward
Finding the right question was most of the work. Ranking questions on day one, and being willing to drop my own when people told me otherwise, mattered more than any single method. The consent question is the one I'd want answered before this ships. It's a product question as much as an ethics one.
