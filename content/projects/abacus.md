## At a glance
**Role.** User research intern on the Customer Insight and User Research team, which sits in the product team.
**Timeline.** June to August 2026, about two months.
**Methods.** Desk research, a survey of micro-business owners, interviews, and concept tests with a prototype I coded.
**The decision.** Whether to invest in serving micro-business owners, and which owners to build for.

## A note on this page
This work is under NDA, so I can’t share findings, the concept, or product details. I can discuss how I worked: how I scoped an open question, where I changed my mind, and what I would do again. Ask me about any of it in conversation.

## Where I started
The brief was to explore how the company could serve micro-business owners. Nobody had pinned down the problem, and I had about two months. In discovery work with a short clock, the main risk is answering the wrong questions very well.

**On day one I wrote down every question leadership might ask and ranked them by what the decision needed.** Three made the cut. Who has this problem most? What are they trying to get done? Would a concept change how they work? Pricing, features, and feasibility went to the bottom, because none of them could be answered honestly before we knew the need was real.

## Where I changed my mind
Desk research framed the space, the survey showed where to look, and the interviews changed my mind. I can’t say what I heard.

**The most useful moment of the summer came when what people told me stopped fitting the story I had arrived with.** I had to decide whether to trust my plan or the people.

Two ideas from the literature helped me read what I was hearing. Schön’s (1983) reflection-in-action describes how people think while they act, which meant owners might not be able to report their habits in an interview. Polanyi’s (1966) tacit knowledge describes how much of what people know resists being put into words. Both pushed me to ask how owners know things, and to stop asking what features they wanted.

## Testing it with something they could touch
A static concept cannot tell you whether someone will adopt a new habit. So I built a working prototype and tested it with two small groups of owners. Their reactions went beyond what I had planned for, which is the reason to put something real in front of people. The groups were small, so I read the results as a direction and not a verdict.

## The readout
I presented to company leadership, including department heads. The deck moved from the problem to the concept to who it is and isn’t for, and it ended on the questions I had left open.

## Holding the boundary
When I shared the direction, someone asked whether we could widen the scope so the concept looked less narrow. It is a fair question, and I didn’t want to answer it with a flat no. I went back to what the tests said and to the question on the table, which was who to build for first. Widening the scope would have blurred that answer, so we kept the boundary and logged the other groups as a later question.

## Impact
- Leadership reviewed my recommendation and gave the concept a place on the product roadmap for future exploration.
- The recommendation named who to serve and left others out on purpose.
- Demand and desirability were tested before anything was built, so the roadmap decision rested on what owners said and did.

## What I’d carry forward
Finding the right question was most of the work. Ranking questions on day one mattered more to me than any single method, and so did being willing to drop my own questions when people told me something different.

Consent is the question I would want answered before this ships. It is a product decision as much as an ethics one.
