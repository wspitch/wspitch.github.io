## At a glance
**Role.** First author and lead researcher, advised by Mark Zachry, with co-authors on each paper.
**Timeline.** Part of my PhD research.
**Methods.** Think-aloud sessions and interviews with 18 designers, a survey of 30, think-aloud sessions and interviews with 12, and a research-through-design process for the toolkit.
**The decision.** What would help designers weigh values and harms at the start of an AI concept, when changing course is still cheap.

## The question
The first ideas for a product are the ones that stick. They decide what it's for, who it serves, and what it could hurt. But designers usually make those calls with little structured help. Most guidelines show up later, or assume AI expertise designers don't have. I wanted to look at that earliest moment, and then build something for it.

## Watching designers work
Eighteen designers, each with at least two years of experience and prior use of generative AI, spent 20 minutes ideating an AI-powered design tool with the AI tools they already used. Each started from one value they chose. Then I interviewed every one of them for 45 minutes. Four of us coded all 18 transcripts and reconciled every disagreement. Only 4 of the 18 had designed AI products professionally.

I kept it narrow on purpose, with one value and a short session, so I could watch what happened to a single value. That choice also hid how designers handle several values at once, and the paper says so.

## What surprised me
Designers struggled to say what a value should look like in a product, yet they could tell when an AI suggestion felt wrong. Sixteen of 18 couldn't turn a value into features, but one said: *"I couldn't articulate exactly why, but I knew it wasn't right."* That unease was more specific than any value statement I'd collected.

The second surprise was how convincing the AI was. Thirteen of 18 accepted suggestions because they sounded technical and matched the value. One designer said they ended up *"essentially letting one AI define the possibilities for another AI I was trying to design."* Most people chose efficiency, which is the value generative AI serves most fluently, and their sketches converged on similar workflow automation. The designers who stopped to question a suggestion found more problems.

## Building the toolkit, and getting it wrong twice
The toolkit has three parts: an AI Capability Library, 24 Value-Harm Cards, and a Value-Tension Map. It starts from what shouldn't happen, and asks designers to put values and harms side by side.

> Image placeholder: Value-Harm Cards and Value-Tension Map

The first version was a standalone website. I tested it with 30 designers. They rated it very useful (6.47 out of 7), but their likelihood to actually adopt it lagged (5.57), and 26 of 30 hesitated about leaving the tools they already worked in. They liked it and still wouldn't open it. So I rebuilt it inside FigJam and published it on the Figma Community, with a print PDF for workshops.

I'd also given the cards numeric sliders, assuming that putting numbers on values would help. It didn't. 21 of 30 said the numbers felt arbitrary. As one put it, what's the difference between a 5 and a 6 on Trust? I removed the sliders and let discussion do the work.

I tested the redesign with 12 designers across two design briefs, thinking aloud. Those who saw harm cues early made more substantive changes to their concepts than those who saw them late (3.26 vs. 1.84 on average). That's why the toolkit asks about harm at the start of a concept, not at review.

## Impact
- A company building an AI product for recruiting and hiring adopted the toolkit. Nobody asked them to. Community usage is modest, so an unprompted pickup is the strongest sign to me that it works outside a study.
- The toolkit is free on the Figma Community and runs inside FigJam, where designers already work.
- Two first-authored papers at DIS 2026, one on how designers work with generative AI and one on the toolkit, and the groundwork for my dissertation.

## What's next
One value in a short session gave me clarity and hid collisions between values. That's where the Value-Tension Map came from, and it's the question I'm studying next.
