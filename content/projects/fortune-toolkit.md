## What I can share
**Can share.** The problem, how we approached it, how I worked with stakeholders, what it led to, and what I took from it.
**Can’t share.** The words on the toolkit, the results, and anything else data related. All of it is under NDA.

## At a glance
**Role.** Contract UX researcher on a team of 5 researchers, working directly with Google stakeholders.
**Question.** Which words stay in a company-wide toolkit for measuring how people see Google’s products?
**Method.** A survey across 5 hardware product types with removal rules set before any results, word-use and confusion analysis in R, and thematic coding of open responses.
**Why a survey.** Validating a company-wide instrument needed breadth across product types, which interviews could not provide.
**Observed.** Most of the toolkit held up. Some words were confusing, some meant different things to different people, and some told us little even when people chose them often.
**Interpreted.** How often people chose a word did not show that it was useful.
**Decided.** Cut and add words with a written rationale for each, and the revised toolkit was adopted across the organization.

## Why a measurement instrument deserves scrutiny
Teams used the toolkit to understand how people felt about Google’s hardware and about Google as a company. The words had come from product literature, ads, and customer reviews, which reflects how companies talk about products and not how customers do.

**A weak item quietly distorts every study that uses it.** A single poor choice repeats across every team that relies on the toolkit, so validating the instrument was worth more than running another study with it.

## Working with stakeholders
Teams across the company relied on the toolkit, so a removal changed their tools. Each cut needed a reason someone could accept without having been in the room. We wrote a rationale for every word, and because the rules were set before the results, we could show stakeholders why and not only what.

The cut that drew the most discussion was a word that had been used heavily in past results, and the concern about breaking comparisons with older studies was legitimate. I didn’t argue that the word was bad. I showed how people actually used it, acknowledged the cost to trend comparisons, and proposed a short note in future reports about what changed.

> Naming the tradeoff openly made it easier for stakeholders to agree.

## How we tested the list
We tested the whole word list at once, across several types of hardware. People picked words to describe the company and the products, chose words for a set of design goals, flagged the words they found confusing, and told us what was missing. I analyzed word use and confusion in R and coded the suggestions thematically.

We set the removal rules before we saw any results, so decisions would follow criteria and not taste. We made calls about the list and never about an individual product.

## What the validation showed
Most of the toolkit held up, and a smaller set of words didn’t. Some were confusing, some meant different things to different people, and some told us little even when people chose them often.

> Popularity was not evidence of usefulness.

We also added a few words that several people suggested independently and left out one-off phrasing.

## Impact
- Every change to the toolkit came with a written rationale.
- My research manager told me the toolkit has been adopted widely by designers and researchers across the organization for hardware product lines, especially the recent smart-home devices.

## What I ruled out
- **Cutting words by taste or by how often people chose them.** We set removal rules before we saw any results, because popularity was not evidence of usefulness.
- **Arguing that a heavily used word was bad.** I showed how people actually used it, acknowledged the cost to comparisons with older studies, and proposed a short note in future reports.
- **Making calls about individual products.** We made calls about the list and never about a product.

## What I’d carry forward
Be explicit about what the evidence cannot support. Because we kept our claims to the toolkit and away from individual products, everything else we reported was easier to trust.

A cleaner vocabulary also does not prevent misuse. A good next step would be guidance on how to apply a word to a product.
