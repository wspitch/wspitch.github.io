## At a glance
**Role.** UX researcher on a team of five researchers, working directly with stakeholders.
**Timeline.** 2021 to 2022.
**Methods.** A survey study with consumers across five hardware product types, word-use and confusion analysis in R, and thematic coding of open responses.
**The decision.** Which words stay in a company-wide tool for measuring how people see its products.

## A note on this page
The company said I can’t share anything data related, and the company and products aren’t named either. I can talk about the problem, how we approached it, what it led to, and what I took from it.

## Why a word list matters
Teams used the list to understand how people felt about the company’s hardware and the company behind it. It had been built from product literature, ads, and customer reviews, which is how companies talk about products and not how customers do.

**A weak word quietly bends every study that uses it.** One bad word gets paid for many times over.

## What we did
We tested the whole list at once, across several types of hardware. People picked words to describe the company and the products, chose words for a set of design goals, flagged the words they found confusing, and told us what the list was missing. I analyzed word use and confusion in R and coded the suggestions thematically.

We set the removal rules before we saw any results, so decisions would follow criteria and not taste. We made calls about the list and never about an individual product.

## Working with stakeholders
Teams across the company relied on the list, so a removal changed their tools. Each cut needed a reason someone could accept without having been in the room. We wrote a rationale for every word, and because the rules were set before the results, we could show stakeholders why and not only what.

The cut that drew the most discussion was a word that had been used heavily in past results. The worry was reasonable: would removing it break comparisons with older studies? I didn’t argue that the word was bad. I showed how people actually used it and offered a short note in future reports about what changed, so anyone comparing across lists would know.

## What the review taught us
Most of the list held up, and a smaller set of words didn’t. Some were confusing, some meant different things to different people, and some told us little even when people chose them often. **Popular didn’t mean useful.** We also added a few words that several people suggested independently and skipped one-off phrasing.

## Impact
- Every change to the list came with a written reason.
- My research manager told me teams across the organization adopted the revised toolkit.

## What I’d carry forward
Say what the evidence can’t support. Because we kept our claims to the list and away from individual products, everything else we reported was easier to trust.

A cleaner vocabulary also doesn’t prevent misuse. A good next step would be guidance on how to apply a word to a product.
